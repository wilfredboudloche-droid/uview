Relative References 101
Understanding how cell addresses shift when copied

📋 The club budget spreadsheet uses relative references — addresses like B2 that automatically adjust when you copy a formula to another cell.

Cell C2 has the formula =A2+B2. When you copy it down to C3, the row numbers shift by 1.

💡

Key concept: In a relative reference, both the column letter and row number adjust based on where you paste the formula. Think of it as "same relative position."

Locking It Down: Absolute References
Using $ signs to freeze cell addresses

📋 The tax rate lives in cell B1 and every row needs to reference it. But when you copy the formula down, B1 drifts to B2, B3… The fix? Make it absolute: $B$1.

💡

Dollar signs lock a reference. $B$1 means "always column B, always row 1" — no matter where you paste the formula.

C2 fx =A2*B1 ← 🐛 Bug! B1 drifts when copied down
ABC

The Best of Both: Mixed References
Lock only the column OR only the row

📋 In a multiplication table, you need the row header to stay in column A (lock the column) and the column header to stay in row 1 (lock the row). This is a mixed reference.

💡

$A1 = column A is locked, row shifts.
A$1 = column shifts, row 1 is locked.
The $ always locks what comes right after it.

The F4 Power Shortcut
Cycle through reference types with one key

📋 Instead of typing dollar signs manually, press F4 while your cursor is on a cell reference to cycle through all four types!

Fill Handle & AutoFill Magic
Drag to copy formulas and patterns

📋 The Fill Handle is the tiny square at the bottom-right corner of a selected cell. Drag it down or across to autofill formulas and patterns!

If C2 contains =A2*$B$1 and you drag the fill handle from C2 down to C4, Sheets copies the formula and adjusts the relative parts automatically.
